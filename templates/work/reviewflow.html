ReviewFlow is a React + Supabase application that helps people write better Google reviews by combining structured input with AI-assisted wording — while preserving the user's own judgment. The portfolio evidence is about how I measured the AI's output quality, not about a deployed product with users.

View on GitHub Working prototype — local/demo environment · no verified public deployment Back to Proof of Work

Context

People want to leave better Google reviews. Reviews written in a hurry are usually brief, vague, or hard to parse. People often know what they want to say but struggle to get the wording right — especially when the experience was complex, nuanced, or emotionally charged.

The deeper problem isn't "write me a review." It's: help me capture what I actually experienced, in a form that's useful to other readers, without making me sound like something I'm not.

What this is

An AI-assisted review-writing tool built as a real React + Supabase application. Not a hypothetical. Not a design doc. It runs — locally if you have the credentials, and the architecture, product decisions, and quality measurement are all documented.

What this is not

It is not a live product with users. It is not a commercial venture. The portfolio evidence is about product thinking and AI quality measurement — not about customer adoption, revenue, GTM, scale, or any production outcome.

Problem

Surface problem: People leave quick, unhelpful reviews. A review tool could help them write better ones.

Deeper problem: Writing a review is a judgment call, not a text-generation task. If you hand the judgment to the AI, you've changed what the tool is — from a review tool to a content generator. The harder problem is keeping the user's judgment intact while improving the output.

Core tension

How do you use AI to improve review writing without making the AI the author of the judgment?

This tension shaped the entire product direction: the AI's role is wording, not judgment. The user's role is the substance. Rating preservation, factual guardrails, and structured input were all downstream consequences of this single decision.

Why It Mattered

AI-assisted writing is becoming common, and most of it treats "better output" as "more impressive output." That framing produces reviews that sound polished and say nothing — or worse, say things the user didn't actually experience.

The product-thinking question worth pursuing was narrower and harder: can you build an AI-assisted tool where the AI genuinely helps, the user stays in charge, and you can measure whether the AI is actually doing what you intended?

Why this is a good portfolio case

It's a real product, built by me, with real quality measurement. It shows AI product integrity thinking — the hardest part of AI product work is knowing whether the AI is helping or just sounding good. The evidence I can show is about that question, not about users.

Users / Assumptions

Assumption: someone who cares about leaving a good review is a reasonable user for this tool. Someone writing a review for a restaurant, a visit, a service, or a product — someone who wants the review to be useful to other readers.

What I assumed about the user: they have an experience they want to capture, they can rate it, they can describe what stood out, and they want the text to be clear without losing their voice.

Hypothesis — not validated with real users

There is no user research behind this. No interviews. No surveys. No measured audience. The user model is a reasonable inference, nothing more. This is an honest gap, not a hidden one.

Product Framing

The product question, stated narrowly: how might we help someone write a better Google review without making the AI the author of their judgment?

The product principle, stated once and used everywhere: AI can improve how you say it. It cannot decide what happened.

The principle is the throughline

This single line is the most important thing in the case study. Everything downstream — rating preservation, factual guardrails, structured input, the editable draft, the manual Google handoff, the disclosure — is a consequence of this principle.

The framing also implicitly defined what the product was not: not a content generator, not a review spinner, not an auto-poster, not a thing that lets the AI decide the rating or the facts.

Discovery / Research

The discovery work was less "user research" and more "what would a responsible AI-assisted review tool actually need to do?" — which is a product-formulation exercise, not a research exercise. The framing came from the core principle, not from personas or interviews.

Observed

Structured input matters: a review written from a blank box and a review built from rating + visit type + what stood out + what to tell others are different outcomes. The structure shapes the content.

Observed

AI output quality is measurable. You can design checks and run them against outputs. Whether those checks are good checks is itself a question — and that's where the evaluator and the corrected evaluator come in.

Hypothesis

A user who gets a structured input + AI-assisted wording + editable draft + manual handoff will leave a better review than one who writes from a blank box. Not tested.

Prioritization

The MVP was deliberately small. The prioritization document separates what was Must Have from what was Should Have and what was explicitly Out of Scope. This matters because it shows the product boundary was thought through, not improvised.

Verified

Must Have: structured input, rating preservation, factual guardrails, editable draft, manual Google handoff, transparency, basic usable UI.

Verified

Out of Scope (explicit): direct Google posting, analytics, authentication, user accounts, multi-business support, native mobile, fabricated social proof, "Powered by AI" badge, guaranteed AI accuracy.

The out-of-scope list is as important as the in-scope list. It shows I was thinking about what this product should not do, which is often where product judgment shows up.

Solution

The solution has four moving parts, and they're arranged deliberately:

  1. Structured input — rating, visit type, what stood out, what to tell others, optional staff name, optional context. The input shapes what the AI works with.
  2. AI-assisted wording — the AI takes the structured input and produces a short draft and a detailed draft. It does not change the rating. It does not invent facts.
  3. Editable draft — the user can edit before copying. The AI is a first draft, not final text.
  4. Manual Google handoff — the user copies the text to Google manually. No auto-posting. No pretending the AI posted the review.

A StatsNote instead of fabricated stats

The app originally had a "stats" panel that showed fabricated numbers. That was replaced with a StatsNote component — a quiet disclosure that says the numbers were placeholders and the product is not live. This is the kind of product-integrity decision that the portfolio is trying to show.

Architecture / Implementation

Frontend: React + Vite + TypeScript + shadcn/ui. The components (ReviewForm, ResultsScreen, StatsNote) are real. The structured input is real. The editable draft is real.

AI gateway: a Supabase Deno edge function that calls Gemini 2.5 Flash through the Lovable AI gateway. The edge function produces structured tool-call output — the AI responds with the short and detailed drafts in a defined shape, which is why the frontend can work with it deterministically.

Implementation note: the app was scaffolded with AI assistance (Lovable). I've been explicit about that. The product decisions, the quality measurement, the MVP boundary, the disclosure, and the honest gaps are mine.

Validation

Validation in this case means "does the AI output actually do what we said it would?" — not "did users like it?" The first is measurable; the second is not evidenced here.

Verified

15-case regression dataset: synthetic test cases covering 1–5 stars, multiple visit types, with/without staff name, positive/negative/mixed experiences. Real file.

Verified

247 deterministic prompt checks, 247/247 passed: the evaluator runs a set of named checks against every case. All 247 pass. The test exits clean. The results file exists.

Verified

Output evaluator: a second evaluator that examines the actual AI output text for rating match, invented detail, marketing/superlative language, overall strict pass, format compliance. The evaluator is real. The results are real.

Verified

15 historical outputs (2026-09-10) evaluated with the corrected evaluator: 47% rating match, 0% invented detail, 60% marketing/superlative, 27% overall strict pass, 100% format compliance.

Not evidenced

The historical outputs are a point-in-time snapshot. There is no current live AI pipeline I can point to. The evaluation evidence is real, but its subject is a closed historical set.

Not evidenced

No live AI validation occurred for the Phase 11 intervention.

Metrics

The metrics plan is a design, not a measurement. It defines what would matter if the product were live: North Star, activation, funnel, quality and safety, guardrail metrics, business metrics. All proposed.

Proposed

North Star: a good review written. Activation: first review drafted. Funnel: structured input entered → AI draft generated → draft edited → copy to clipboard → manual Google submission. Quality: rating preserved, facts from user input, no invented detail. Guardrail: inappropriate output rate. Business: review submission rate.

Not evidenced

No metric has been measured. The product is not live with users. The metrics plan is evidence of thinking about measurement, not evidence of measurement.

Outcomes

Honest outcomes

Verified

The product was built. It runs locally with credentials. The AI output quality is measurable, and I measured it against a historical set of 15 outputs: 47% rating match, 0% invented detail, 27% strict pass, 100% format compliance.

Not evidenced

No real users. No live production AI. No measured adoption or outcomes. No GTM. No scale. No launch. No growth. No customer validation. No measured impact on review quality in the wild.

Hypothesis

The Phase 11 prompt tightening may improve live AI behavior. It passes deterministic checks (247/247), but the live before/after was never genuinely measurable — no LOVABLE_API_KEY or Supabase credentials in the environment. beforeVsAfterGenuinelyMeasurable: false. The intervention is documented honestly as unproven.

Learnings

1. AI product integrity is a real and measurable problem. The hardest part of building an AI-assisted product is knowing whether the AI is actually helping or just sounding good. You can design checks for that, and you can run them.

2. Measurement artifacts are easy to introduce. The corrected evaluator found three measurement artifacts (case sensitivity, superlative word forms, false positive from the word "out"). The correction made measurement more accurate. The Phase 9 number (53%) looked like improvement; it was actually an evaluator artifact. Correct measurement matters.

3. Honest documentation is itself a product discipline. The Phase 11 intervention record was written to say plainly "live validation did not occur" and to avoid claiming improvement. That kind of honesty is not optional in a portfolio built on evidence.

4. Structured input is not just UX. It shapes what the AI can work with and what it cannot invent. The structure is a constraint that makes the product more honest.

What I Would Do Next

  1. Live-evaluate the Phase 11 prompt tightening against the same metric framework with real AI credentials. Before/after genuinely measurable, not just deterministic-check-passing.
  2. Validate the user model. Talk to real people who write reviews. Find out whether the structured input and AI-assisted wording actually help.
  3. Define and measure the North Star. If the product were live, measure what matters — good review written, draft edited, manual Google submission.
  4. Build the analytics. The metrics plan defines what to measure. The next step is to actually instrument it and measure it.
  5. Consider the GTM honestly. This is a small tool for a real but narrow job. The honest question is whether there's a real audience for it, not whether it can be scaled.
Proposed

None of the above has been done. They are next steps, not evidence.

Key takeaway

AI can improve how you say it. It cannot decide what happened. If you build an AI-assisted product, the first hard problem is knowing whether the AI is actually helping — and you can measure that. The second hard problem is not claiming you've solved the first one when you haven't.
