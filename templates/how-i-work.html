Product Operating System

The way I work through a product problem.

Eight moves. Not a decorative diagram — a real lifecycle I apply across every project on this portfolio. Each stage is anchored to something I've actually done, or honestly marked where evidence does not yet exist. The thread is the same across all three products: define the problem, make trade-offs explicit, build measurement before the outcome, verify what I claim, and be honest about gaps.

01

Discover

Observed / Hypothesis

Find the real problem before proposing a solution. Disambiguate. Separate facts from assumptions from inferences from unknowns. Name the customer and the job.

Before I build anything, I try to understand what problem I'm actually solving. That sounds obvious, but most product work skips it — the problem is assumed, the solution is proposed, and the measure is an afterthought.

On Unrot, the problem-framing research explicitly separates known facts from assumptions, inferences, and unknowns — and organizes seven candidate reasons for low D1 retention into categories instead of picking one and running with it. Four JTBD hypotheses and seven intervention hypotheses are documented, each labeled as a hypothesis, not validated research.

On ReviewFlow, the discovery wasn't user research — it was product formulation: what would a responsible AI-assisted review tool actually need to do? The answer shaped everything downstream. On Experiment Tracker, the gap between running an experiment and recording what I concluded from it was observed from personal practice — not from external user research.

Gap: no user research, no interviews, no measured audience, no validated JTBD
02

Decide

Verified

Make explicit trade-offs. Prefer the smallest thing that closes the most important gap. Say why you're not doing the other things.

Product thinking is mostly decision-making under constraints. The interesting part isn't the decision — it's the reasoning. What options did I consider? What trade-offs did I accept? What would I do differently?

ReviewFlow's product decisions document records 8 decisions — structured input vs blank box, anti-hallucination constraints, rating preservation, short+detailed drafts, editable draft, manual Google handoff, no database, no auth — each with options considered, trade-offs, confidence, and a validation approach.

Experiment Tracker's PRODUCT_BRIEF evaluates seven candidate capabilities against a single question: what's the smallest thing that closes the loop? Conclusion capture won. Charts, search/filter, authentication, cloud, AI features, templates, artifact linking — all evaluated, all deliberately not chosen for v1. The "why not prioritized" column is where the judgment shows up.

03

Define

Verified / Proposed

Turn the decision into a defined product: what it is, what it is not, what's in scope, what's out of scope, what success looks like.

Once I understand the problem, I define the product. Not the implementation — the product. What job does it do? What's in scope? What's out of scope? What's the smallest thing that actually solves the problem?

ReviewFlow's MVP prioritization separates Must Have from Should Have from Out of Scope — and the Out of Scope list is as important as the in-scope list. It shows I was thinking about what the product should not do: no direct Google posting, no analytics, no authentication, no user accounts, no multi-business, no native mobile, no fabricated social proof, no "Powered by AI" badge, no guaranteed AI accuracy.

Unrot's measurement model defines what to measure before anything is validated: a metric hierarchy (North Star, activation, funnel, quality/safety, guardrail, business), a 7-stage funnel (first_session_started → onboarding_completed → plan_viewed → lesson_started → lesson_completed → reminder_enabled → D1_returned), must-have instrumentation (eventName, propertiesJson, occurredAt, sessionId, route, userId). The metrics plan is defined — the outcome is not measured.

Experiment Tracker's PRODUCT_BRIEF defines the problem, the user (inferred), the JTBDs (hypothesized), the product question, and the scope — and evaluates seven alternatives against it.

Gap: formal PRDs, user stories, and acceptance criteria as distinct artifacts not yet evidenced
04

Build

Verified

Build the thing. Keep it real. Don't pretend it's more than it is. If it's AI-assisted, say so. If it's local-only, say so. If it's a prototype, say so.

I build products that actually run. ReviewFlow is a real React + Supabase application — not a design concept, not a Figma file. It has components, an edge function, and a CI workflow. It runs locally with credentials. I was explicit that it was scaffolded with AI assistance — the product decisions, the measurement, the MVP boundary, the disclosure, and the honest gaps are mine.

Unrot is a real Next.js 16 + PostgreSQL + Prisma application with auth, sessions, learning paths, lessons, progress tracking, reminder preferences, and an analytics dashboard that computes from a real database. It's production-shaped, not a toy. Build, lint, and typecheck pass. ESLint 0 errors / 0 warnings.

Experiment Tracker is a real Flask + SQLite application with 22 passing tests and a 17-category verification report. It runs. It's verified. It's intentionally small.

Gap: all three products built solo — cross-functional engineering collaboration not evidenced on the portfolio site
05

Launch

Not evidenced

Make the product available to real people. Beta, rollout, GTM, stakeholder communication. Distribution is a product decision, not an afterthought.

This is the stage where the portfolio's evidence currently stops. All three products are working prototypes in local/demo environments. None has a verified public deployment. None has a measured adoption, a growth story, or a GTM strategy.

I'm honest about that. A launch isn't something you can fake with a README line. The gap between "built and verified" and "launched and learned from" is real, and I'm not claiming it closed.

Gap: no launch, no beta, no GTM, no rollout, no distribution — this is the next lifecycle stage to close
06

Measure

Verified / Proposed / Hypothesis

Define what you care about before you claim you've changed it. Design the measurement system. Run the checks. Say what you can and can't measure.

The hardest part of product work — especially AI product work — is knowing whether what you built is actually doing what you intended. You can't tell that without measurement.

ReviewFlow's quality measurement is the strongest example: 247 deterministic prompt checks (all pass), an output evaluator, 15 historical outputs evaluated against five dimensions (rating match, invented detail, marketing/superlative, strict pass, format compliance). The evaluator was corrected when it introduced measurement artifacts — case-insensitive visit-type matching, extended superlative word forms, and removal of a false positive from the word "out." Phase 9's 53% looked like improvement; it was actually a measurement error. The correction made measurement more accurate. The 15 outputs were evaluated against a closed historical snapshot (2026-09-10) — not a live production pipeline.

Unrot's measurement model is the other pole: a metric hierarchy, a 7-stage funnel, must-have instrumentation, guardrails, a D1 retention calculator (calendar-day logic in the user's configured IANA timezone with UTC fallback, excluding demo_return_previewed, with a cohort completeness rule), an analytics dashboard that computes from a real database and shows "not enough real cohort data yet" honestly, a baseline and target shown as context labels, and an A/B experiment plan with power analysis (~650–700 per variant, ~1,300–1,400 total), 2-week duration + 2-day cooldown, and decision rules (win/inconclusive/loss).

Experiment Tracker's proposed usage metric — experiments_with_conclusion / completed_experiments — is a way to know whether the conclusion feature is actually being used. It's proposed, not measured. The product is local-first and personal by design; there is no telemetry.

Gap: no measured outcomes — ReviewFlow evaluated against historical outputs only; Unrot D1 outcome not measured; Experiment Tracker usage metric not measured
07

Learn

Verified / Hypothesis

What happened? What worked? What did not? At what confidence level? Learning is where verification meets honesty — and it's where the discipline shows up.

Verification is where the discipline shows up. It's not "does it run?" — it's "did you claim what you can support?"

When the ReviewFlow evaluator introduced measurement artifacts, I corrected it. When the Phase 11 intervention couldn't be live-validated, I documented that honestly — saying plainly that live validation did not occur and that the before/after was not genuinely measurable (beforeVsAfter GenuinelyMeasurable: false). That kind of honesty is not optional in a portfolio built on evidence.

Experiment Tracker's 17-category verification report is an independent post-implementation review, not a self-assessment: database integrity, conclusion CRUD, one-conclusion-per-experiment constraint, cascade deletion, run detail route, compare workflow, error handling, timestamps, HTML generation, accessibility by code review, security by code review. 22 tests. Real report.

Unrot's analytics dashboard shows a real state — no closed cohorts means no D1 returners to report. That's the truth. Fabricating a number would be the opposite of the product discipline this case is trying to show.

Learning is folded into verification. I don't treat "learn" as a separate stage — learning happens when you check what you claimed and find out whether it's true. The learnings from ReviewFlow's corrected evaluator: measurement artifacts are easy to introduce, and a number that looks like improvement can actually be a measurement error. The learnings from Unrot's analytics: honest "not enough data" is better than invented data. The learnings from Experiment Tracker: don't claim what you haven't measured, and code review is not browser testing.

Gap: outcome-based learning is not possible without measured outcomes — learnings are about the machinery, not about results
08

Iterate

Proposed

Feed what you learned back into the next decision. Change the product. Propose the next experiment. Update the roadmap. The lifecycle doesn't end at launch — it loops.

Each case study closes with a "What I Would Do Next" section listing specific next steps — never pretending they've been done.

On ReviewFlow: live-evaluate the Phase 11 prompt tightening against the same metric framework with real AI credentials; validate the user model with real people who write reviews; define and measure the North Star if the product were live; build the analytics; consider the GTM honestly — whether there's a real audience for a small tool for a real but narrow job.

On Unrot: validate the JTBD hypotheses with real users; run the A/B experiment the way the plan describes; measure the D1 outcome with a real cohort; instrument the full funnel in production; build the cohort dashboard with real cohort data.

On Experiment Tracker: validate the user model with real people who run experiments locally; measure the usage metric if the product were in use; add search/filter as the first v2 candidate; add artifact upload; add visualization as a nice-to-have.

Gap: no iteration has been executed yet — these are proposed next steps, not completed ones

Cross-functional product thinking

How I approach product work across functions.

Product decisions don't happen in one discipline. They happen at the intersection of engineering, data, design, business, and quality — and the right input changes depending on where you are in the lifecycle. This is how I approach that intersection. It's descriptive of my operating approach, not a claim of specific collaborations.

Engineering
  • Feasibility and technical constraints
  • Architecture fit and implementation cost
  • Reliability and maintainability
  • What's realistic to build and maintain
Data / Analytics
  • Define measurable outcomes before building
  • Instrumentation and event design
  • Metric definitions with explicit boundaries
  • Experiment design and decision rules
  • Distinguish signal from insufficient evidence
Design / UX
  • User flow and task completion
  • Usability and friction points
  • Accessibility as a baseline, not an afterthought
  • Interaction trade-offs and cognitive load
Business / GTM
  • Customer and value proposition
  • Business constraints and viability
  • Prioritization against the real problem
  • Launch considerations and distribution
QA / Support
  • Acceptance criteria and edge cases
  • Validation before claiming
  • Feedback loops from real use
  • What breaks, and what that tells you

How the functions map to the lifecycle

Cross-functional input is not uniform across the eight moves. It concentrates where it matters and recedes where it doesn't.

DISCOVER

User and business context, evidence, and the real problem.

DEFINE

Problem, requirements, success criteria, and what's out of scope.

DECIDE

Trade-offs across user, business, data, and technical constraints.

BUILD

Engineering, design, and QA execution — the implementation stage.

LAUNCH

Business, GTM, and stakeholder communication — currently the portfolio's evidence gap.

MEASURE

Analytics, instrumentation, outcome measurement, and experiment design.

LEARN

Feedback, evidence, and what the data actually says — at what confidence.

ITERATE

Feed what you learned back into the next decision. The lifecycle loops.

Honest scope

This section describes how I approach cross-functional product work. It is not a claim that these projects involved structured collaboration with engineering, data, design, business, or QA teams — the portfolio does not currently evidence that. What it does evidence is that I think in these terms when I define, build, measure, and verify products.

The move in one line

I turn ambiguous problems into products that can be defined, built, measured, and verified — and I'm unusually careful about what I claim along the way. The evidence labels on this portfolio aren't decoration. They're the system I use to keep the claims honest.
