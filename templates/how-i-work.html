How I Think & How I Work

How I Think

The way I work through a product problem.

Six moves. Each one is grounded in the actual work on this portfolio — not generic PM methodology. Some are observed in my products, some are inferred from how I've worked, some are proposals. The evidence labels tell you which is which.

Find the real problem before you propose a solution. Disambiguate. Separate facts from assumptions from inferences from unknowns.

Before I build anything, I try to understand what problem I'm actually solving. That sounds obvious, but most product work skips it — the problem is assumed, the solution is proposed, and the measure is an afterthought.

In practice, this means separating what I know from what I'm guessing. On Unrot, the problem-framing research explicitly separates known facts from assumptions, inferences, and unknowns — and organizes seven candidate reasons for low D1 retention into categories instead of picking one and running with it.

On ReviewFlow, the discovery wasn't user research — it was product formulation: what would a responsible AI-assisted review tool actually need to do? The answer shaped everything downstream.

Observed — problem framing as a deliberate step, not an assumption. Hypothesis — the JTBD list and intervention hypotheses for Unrot are good guesses, not validated research.

Define the product before you build it. What is it? What is it not? What's the smallest thing that does the job?

Once I understand the problem, I define the product. Not the implementation — the product. What job does it do? What's in scope? What's out of scope? What's the smallest thing that actually solves the problem?

ReviewFlow's MVP prioritization separates Must Have from Should Have from Out of Scope — and the Out of Scope list is as important as the in-scope list. It shows I was thinking about what the product should not do.

Experiment Tracker's PRODUCT_BRIEF evaluates seven candidate capabilities against a single question: what's the smallest thing that closes the loop? Conclusion capture won. Charts, search, auth, cloud, AI features, templates, artifacts — all evaluated, all deliberately not chosen for v1.

Verified — MVP prioritization and product brief documents exist. The trade-offs are explicit.

Make explicit trade-offs. Prefer the smallest thing that closes the loop. Say why you're not doing the other things.

Product thinking is mostly decision-making under constraints. The interesting part isn't the decision — it's the reasoning. What options did I consider? What trade-offs did I accept? What would I do differently?

ReviewFlow's product decisions document records 8 decisions — structured input vs blank box, anti-hallucination constraints, rating preservation, short+detailed drafts, editable draft, manual Google handoff, no database, no auth — each with options, trade-offs, confidence, and a validation approach.

Experiment Tracker's prioritization is the same move at a higher level: seven candidates, one chosen, six deliberately not. The "why not prioritized" column is where the judgment shows up.

Verified — decision records exist with explicit options and trade-offs. Proposed — the prioritization move itself could be formalized further as a reusable framework.

Build the thing. Keep it real. Don't pretend it's more than it is. If it's AI-assisted, say so. If it's local-only, say so. If it's a prototype, say so.

I build products that actually run. ReviewFlow is a real React + Supabase application — not a design concept, not a Figma file, not a "wouldn't it be cool if." It has components, it has an edge function, it has a CI workflow. It runs locally with credentials.

Unrot is a real Next.js 16 + PostgreSQL + Prisma application with auth, sessions, learning paths, lessons, progress tracking, reminder preferences, and an analytics dashboard that computes from a real database. It's production-shaped, not a toy.

Experiment Tracker is a real Flask + SQLite application with 22 passing tests and a 17-category verification report. It runs. It's verified. It's intentionally small.

And I'm honest about how they were built. ReviewFlow was scaffolded with AI assistance — I've said that clearly. The product decisions, the measurement, the MVP boundary, the disclosure, the honest gaps are mine.

Verified — all three products run. All three have real architecture documents. All three have real implementation evidence. Observed — AI-assisted implementation disclosed where it happened. Not hidden.

Define what you care about before you claim you've changed it. Design the measurement system. Run the checks. Say what you can and can't measure.

The hardest part of product work — especially AI product work — is knowing whether what you built is actually doing what you intended. You can't tell that without measurement.

ReviewFlow's quality measurement is the strongest example: 247 deterministic prompt checks, an output evaluator, 15 historical outputs evaluated against five dimensions (rating match, invented detail, marketing/superlative, strict pass, format compliance). The evaluator was corrected when it introduced measurement artifacts. The correction made measurement more accurate — Phase 9's 53% looked like improvement; it was actually a measurement error.

Unrot's measurement model is the other pole: a metric hierarchy, a 7-stage funnel, must-have instrumentation, guardrails, a D1 retention calculator, an analytics dashboard that computes from a real database and shows "not enough real cohort data yet" honestly, a baseline and target shown as context labels, an A/B experiment plan with power analysis and decision rules. All designed before any outcome was claimed.

Verified — measurement artifacts exist for both ReviewFlow and Unrot. The corrected evaluator is real. The analytics dashboard is real. The experiment plan is real. Not evidenced — no measured outcomes from either product. The D1 retention outcome was never measured. The AI quality scores are against historical outputs, not a live pipeline.

Check what you claimed. Correct your measurements when they're wrong. Document what you haven't validated. Learn from the gap between what you built and what you can prove.

Verification is where the discipline shows up. It's not "does it run?" — it's "did you claim what you can support?"

Experiment Tracker's 17-category verification report is an independent post-implementation review: database integrity, conclusion CRUD, one-conclusion-per-experiment constraint, cascade deletion, run detail route, compare workflow, error handling, timestamps, HTML generation, accessibility by code review, security by code review. 22 tests. Real report.

When the ReviewFlow evaluator introduced measurement artifacts, I corrected it. When the Phase 11 intervention couldn't be live-validated, I documented that honestly — saying plainly that live validation did not occur and that the before/after was not genuinely measurable. That kind of honesty is not optional in a portfolio built on evidence.

Learning is folded into verification. I don't treat "learn" as a separate stage — learning happens when you check what you claimed and find out whether it's true. The learning from ReviewFlow's corrected evaluator: measurement artifacts are easy to introduce, and a number that looks like improvement can actually be a measurement error. The learning from Unrot's analytics: honest "not enough data" is better than invented data. The learning from Experiment Tracker: don't claim what you haven't measured.

Verified — verification reports exist. Corrections were made and documented. Honest gaps are stated. Hypothesis — the Phase 11 intervention improvement is a hypothesis, not a validated result. The JTBD list is a hypothesis, not validated research. Proposed — the evidence-label system itself is a proposal for how to think about evidence status across all product work.
The move in one line

I turn ambiguous problems into products that can be defined, built, measured, and verified — and I'm unusually careful about what I claim along the way. The evidence labels on this portfolio aren't decoration. They're the system I use to keep the claims honest.
